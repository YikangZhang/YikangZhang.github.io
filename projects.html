Program 1 · Main program

Partners in Crime

Why do young people break rules together?

Much offending by teenagers and young adults happens in company. Partners in Crime studies what happens between friends at the moment a decision is made: the nudges they give, the favors they owe, and the company they choose to keep. We built two games for this, Sardegna (a virtual-reality quiz played with a real friend) and Eccellenza (an online investment game), and combine them with surveys and scenario studies.

Co-led with Jean-Louis van Gelder · Max Planck Institute for the Study of Crime, Security and Law

Sardegna: is it the company or the nudge?

Do friends lead us into rule-breaking just by being there, or by egging us on?

With Jean-Louis van Gelder and Wade C. Jacobsen · virtual-reality study in MAXLab, MPI-CSL

Preprint on CrimRxiv →

The question

Company or encouragement?

Peers may sway us simply by being around, or by actively pointing out a chance to break the rules and pushing us to take it, which criminologists call instigation. These are hard to tell apart in everyday life, and lab studies rarely involve real friends.

What we built

Sardegna: a VR quiz played with a real friend

Pairs of friends meet as look-alike avatars in a shared virtual room and play a quiz together for a cash prize.

Inside Sardegna: a quiz question with a timer (A), the answer buttons (B), the frosted screen that separates the friends in the final round (C), and the shared table with the friend's avatar (D).

Key finding

Players felt they were really there, together

Players reported a clear sense of being in the virtual room, and those paired with a friend felt they shared it with them. This shows that VR can recreate the experience of being with a friend convincingly enough to study peer influence experimentally.

Key finding

The nudge, not the company, made the difference

In the final round, a short message encouraging players to cheat tripled cheating. A friend who was merely present made no difference.

Why it matters

A shared tool for peer research

Sardegna offers causal evidence among real friends that a minimal prompt is enough to trigger rule-breaking. The game is available to other researchers on request, and its messages, rewards and number of peers can be changed to test other ways peers influence each other, across labs and countries.

Choosing company, catching habits

Do we choose company that matches us, and does that choice shape what we do later?

With Jean-Louis van Gelder (MPI-CSL) and Wade C. Jacobsen (University of Maryland)

The question

Choosing company is part of the story

Research on peers and crime usually treats our choice of friends as a nuisance to control for. We studied it as a decision in its own right: whether to stay with or leave someone we have just watched break the rules.

How we study it

Stay or switch?

In two online experiments, 591 young adults played an investment game in which they could cheat for money, next to a partner (in fact a computer program) who always played honestly or always cheated. After every ten rounds they chose to stay with that partner or switch.

The Eccellenza game: players choose an investment, then report which payoff they chose. Only two of nine options pay out big, so reporting big payoffs far more often than chance reveals cheating.

Key finding

We keep company that mirrors us

Players who cheated more at the start, or scored lower on honesty, tended to stay with a dishonest partner and leave an honest one; more honest players did the reverse. The sorting that friendship studies see over months and years appeared within minutes.

Key finding

That company changes behavior

Next to a dishonest partner, players cheated far more.

With a dishonest partner With an honest partner

Estimated cheating (big-payoff reports above the 22% expected by chance), Experiment 1. Partners were assigned at random in block 1; from block 2 on, players had chosen to stay or switch. Experiment 2 showed the same pattern.

Key finding

It carries over once the partner is gone

The longer players had spent with a dishonest partner, the more they cheated in the rounds they played alone right afterwards. Part of the link between personal tendencies and cheating ran through these choices: tendency, then who you stay with, then what you are exposed to, then what you do.

Why it matters

Selection and influence are one chain

Choosing company and being influenced by it are not rival explanations but linked steps. Where people start also matters: switching was rare, so those who began with a dishonest partner spent about twice as long with one. Chance encounters and our own choices together decide how much bad influence we meet.

Forever in your debt

Does a favor make us follow a partner's lead in breaking rules, or simply keep us around?

With Sophie Hetche and Linda Rosar (MPI-CSL)

The question

Can a favor buy loyalty in rule-breaking?

Classic research shows that small favors make people more willing to comply with later requests. We asked whether this extends to a partner's advice about breaking a rule, and whether a favor makes it harder to walk away from that partner.

How we study it

A real favor between real partners

304 young adults played online in real pairs, with no deception. Each was paired with someone who had given the opposite advice: follow the rule, or take money meant for other participants. Some received a favor when their better-off partner shared their larger starting pot. Pairs then decided, block by block, how much to take and whether to stay together.

Key finding

~50%

longer before leaving a partner who had done them a favor

Favors kept partnerships together

A favor made people feel grateful, indebted and obliged toward their partner, and they stayed with that partner longer.

Key finding

Partners pulled each other closer, favor or not

Paired with someone of the opposite view, people moved toward their partner and drifted back once alone. But a favor did not make them follow their partner's advice more closely, contrary to what classic reciprocity research predicts.

Gain-seekers, paired with a rule-follower Rule-followers, paired with a gain-seeker

Average amount taken per decision (GBP), among the 194 participants from all conditions who later left their partner (exploratory). Gain-seekers had advised taking the money; rule-followers had advised against it.

Key finding

Unexpectedly, favor recipients took more

Instead, people who received a favor took more money overall, most clearly when their partner favored taking it. Because takings were shared, some may have seen taking money as a way to repay the partner; as one put it, “I wanted to help us to get more money.” This explanation is still tentative.

Favor recipients were also more likely to describe their choices in terms of shared gain (19% vs 5% of those who started equal).

Why it matters

When kindness enables rule-breaking

A favor did not buy obedience, but it did buy loyalty, and possibly a willingness to bend rules for the partnership. Favors may keep people in risky partnerships and make shared rule-breaking feel like repayment, which marks a boundary for classic ideas about reciprocity.

Which friends can talk us into it?

Which features of a friendship make us go along with a friend's request to break the rules?

With Jean-Louis van Gelder

The question

Not all friends are equal

Theories of crime say friends matter, but not which features of a particular friendship make us go along with a friend's request in the moment, or what that request does to the friendship.

How we study it

Three friends, six scenarios

416 young adults named a close, a moderate and a casual friend and described each friendship. They then imagined each friend asking them to steal, get into a fight or break traffic rules, rated how likely they would be to go along, and said how they would treat the friend afterwards.

Key finding

What the friendship is like matters more than how long it has lasted

Linked to more willingness

Linked to more willingness

How long you have been friends

No clear effect

No clear effect

No clear effect

Comparisons within each person across their three friends, also accounting for self-control, past delinquency and gender. Answers concern imagined situations, not observed behavior.

Key finding

Looking ahead, not looking back

How long a friendship had lasted made no difference; expecting it to last did. People were more willing to go along with friends they saw as a reliable presence in their future. Friends with more power also made the act itself seem more acceptable.

Key finding

Going along is not approving

Saying yes and feeling good about the friend usually went together, but not always. In about 8% of cases, people would go along while planning to think less of the friend or see them less, and many who refused kept the friendship intact. Complying and relating are distinct responses to the same request.

Why it matters

The kind of friendship counts

Classic theories focus on how long and how often we spend time with friends. Our results point instead to a friendship's content: shared rule-breaking, unequal power and the expectation of a lasting bond. These are the relationships where requests to break the rules are hardest to refuse.

Choosing and following the wrong crowd

Does living for the moment make young people more open to peer pressure?

Linda Rosar, PhD candidate, with Jean-Louis van Gelder

The question

Living for the moment

Short-Term Mindsets Theory proposes that many personal risk factors for crime share one root: focusing on immediate rewards rather than long-term consequences. Unlike a fixed trait, this mindset can shift with circumstances.

How we study it

Reviewing the evidence

A structured review of published studies asks whether a short-term mindset makes adolescents more susceptible to delinquent peers, and whether it shapes who they spend time with.

What comes next

From review to experiment

The review will guide an online experiment, and may point to ways of helping young people resist negative peer influence.

Preliminary analyses

When school matters reach the courtroom

What do Dutch youth courts do with what schools tell them about an incident and about the young person?

Based on published judgments from rechtspraak.nl

The question

Two systems, one incident

School information reaches a Dutch youth criminal court in two ways. It can be evidence about what happened: a teacher saw the fight, staff recognized a pupil on camera, or the register shows an absence. Or it can be background about the young person, such as behavior and attendance, usually passed on in a report by youth probation or the Child Protection Board, the agency that advises the court. What do courts do with it?

How we study it

A close reading of published judgments

The main study reads 37 published youth criminal judgments from rechtspraak.nl in full, in English translation. A fixed coding scheme records, for each piece of school information, what the court uses it to establish, whether the court examines it, and through whom the school speaks. Our preliminary analyses applied the same approach to a separate set of judgments. We study the published text only, so we see what courts write, not everything they considered.

Preliminary analyses

Courts explain where a point is disputed

One might expect courts to look closely at school information when deciding guilt and simply accept it when deciding the sentence. Our preliminary analyses point to a different pattern. Courts seem to write reasons about school information where someone disputes the point, at either stage. Where nothing is disputed, it sits in a list of evidence, behind a confession, or inside a professional's report that the court adopts.

Preliminary; the main study will test this.

Preliminary analyses

Giving reasons is not checking the source

When a court does give reasons, it sometimes examines the school source itself: how far away the teacher stood, or how clear the camera images were. In other judgments it takes the school's information as given and reasons about something else. Our preliminary analyses suggest that counting both as "checked" would overstate how often courts look closely at what schools report.

Preliminary; the main study will test this.

Preliminary analyses

At sentencing, the school speaks secondhand

At sentencing, the school rarely seems to speak for itself. Its view arrives as a line in someone else's account, such as an advisory report. The judgments in our preliminary analyses seldom say who at school said it, when, or how the report's author knows. Some lines about schooling may not come from the school at all. The young person's own account of school is seldom recorded.

Preliminary; the main study will test this.

Preliminary analyses

School discipline can cut either way

Schools sometimes respond to a suspicion themselves, by suspending or removing the pupil. In our preliminary analyses, judgments tend to mention this without saying who decided or why. Courts then appear to treat it in different ways. Some count it as a burden already carried and lower the sentence. Others do not take it up at all. Unexamined school information does not always work against the young person.

Preliminary; the main study will test this.

Why it matters

What comes next

Schools are not neutral bystanders. They investigate, discipline and report. If these patterns hold, what a school says can shape a sentence without the judgment showing where it came from, and a school's own punishment may or may not count. The main study will test each pattern on its own set of judgments. It may confirm them or contradict them.

Gasliteracy: when is a memory conflict gaslighting?

How do people tell everyday memory disagreements apart from gaslighting?

With Celine van Golde (University of Sydney)

Preprint on PsyArXiv →

The question

A popular word, a blurry line

Gaslighting, abuse that makes someone doubt their own memory, was Merriam-Webster's word of the year in 2022. Yet partners disagree about what happened all the time. How do people decide when a disagreement is abuse?

How we study it

Recalling a real disagreement

252 adults recalled a time their partner contradicted their memory, defined gaslighting in their own words, and rated how gaslit they had felt. They also described their relationship and personality, and independent coders rated each incident.

Key finding

1 in 4

saw their own memory conflict as gaslighting, even to some extent

Memory conflicts are common; gaslighting labels are not

Nearly two in three eligible participants could recall a memory conflict with their partner. Most saw it as an ordinary disagreement.

Key finding

Observers see some of it, not all

Independent coders agreed only moderately with participants. Relationship quality predicted both, but more strongly participants' own ratings, so people in strained relationships may experience more gaslighting, read ambiguous conflicts that way, or both. People who define gaslighting as deliberate manipulation set a higher bar for using the word.

Why it matters

Telling abuse from ordinary forgetting

Research and public communication need to separate gaslighting from everyday memory fallibility, so that real abuse is recognized without every disagreement being labeled abuse.

From gaslighter to gaslightee

Does it matter whose side of a memory conflict we see?

With Celine van Golde (University of Sydney)

The question

Whose side are you on?

People can often tell gaslighting from ordinary disagreement. But does that depend on whether they picture themselves as the one challenging a partner's memory, or the one being challenged?

How we study it

Four short stories

249 adults read memory-conflict scenarios that were either manipulative (a partner challenges a memory to avoid looking bad and keep control) or an honest disagreement, told from the challenger's side or from the receiving side. They rated each from 1 (not gaslighting) to 8 (definitely gaslighting).

Key finding

Manipulation is easiest to see from the inside

Manipulative scenarios were rated as more gaslighting overall, but mainly when told by the challenger, whose motive was stated. From the receiving side, manipulation and honest disagreement looked much alike.

Key finding

Intent sets the bar

People who defined gaslighting as necessarily deliberate were less likely to call any scenario gaslighting, while those who focused on harm to the victim were more likely to. Relationship quality made no difference; people scoring higher on dark personality traits gave higher ratings to the manipulative challenger's story.

Why it matters

Hard to see from where victims stand

From the receiving end, a partner's manipulative intent is rarely visible, which may be one reason gaslighting is hard to recognize while it is happening. Clear public definitions need to account for that.

Alcohol, bystanders and memory

How does alcohol change what bystanders do, and what witnesses later remember?

Co-led with Sera Wiechert, with Jean-Louis van Gelder (MPI-CSL)

The question

Two questions about a night out

Alcohol may make it harder to notice risk and step in when someone is being sexually harassed. It is also often involved when witnesses later have to say what happened. Little is known about either effect in realistic settings.

How we study it

A pub in virtual reality

Participants, either sober or after drinking alcohol, experience a virtual pub in which sexual harassment unfolds. We record how they react in the moment, then test their memory of the event.

Why it matters

Behavior and memory in one study

Studying both together shows how alcohol shapes whether people help and how reliable their later account is, questions that matter for prevention and for the courtroom.

Theme B · Social influence and rule-breaking

How others' reactions shape rule-breaking

Partners in Crime asks how peers push us toward breaking rules. These projects look at the other side of influence: what happens when others let unfairness slide, when the partner is a machine that never objects, and how friends' norms shape image-based sexual abuse.

Under review Results inside

No bad deed goes punished

What happens to people whose unfair behavior goes unpunished?

With Qian Sun (Suzhou University of Science and Technology)

The question

When unfairness slides

Punishing unfair behavior helps keep fairness norms alive, yet people often let it go. We asked what that tolerance does to the person who acted unfairly.

How we study it

A game of splitting money

200 participants repeatedly decided how to split 100 points with a partner who could accept or reject. Partners either accepted nearly every offer or rejected unfair ones. Before and after, participants made offers to a new partner and rated how socially appropriate different splits were.

Key finding

Getting away with it carries over to the next person

Facing a partner who never punished, participants offered less during the game and later offered less to a new partner too. Those facing a partner who rejected unfair offers stayed fair.

Partner who accepted nearly everything Partner who rejected unfair offers

Average offer, out of 100 points, to a new partner whose response they did not see. During the game itself, offers averaged 33 versus 49 points.

Key finding

18% vs 4%

of later offers to a new partner were 20 points or less, after a permissive versus an enforcing partner

A small average shift hides a jump in very unfair offers

Most people kept making fair offers, but very low offers became far more common after facing a partner who let everything slide.

Key finding

Unfair splits start to look acceptable

Participants who met a permissive partner rated moderately unfair splits as more socially appropriate, and became more willing to accept unfair offers themselves.

Why it matters

How fairness norms may erode

Letting unfairness slide affects more than the moment: it changes what the person does next, toward other people, and what they think is acceptable. Repeated across many people and situations, this could slowly wear fairness norms down.

Artificial Enablers

Does dealing with AI that never pushes back make us less fair to people?

With Qian Sun (Suzhou University of Science and Technology)

The question

Partners that never say no

AI systems are built to be helpful and compliant. Unlike people, they do not show disapproval or refuse unfair treatment. What happens to our behavior when a partner never pushes back?

How we study it

Playing with a bot or a person

In two experiments with 593 young adults, planned and registered in advance, participants repeatedly split points with either a human partner or a simple bot (a program, not real AI) that accepted every offer. Afterwards they played with a new human partner and rated how appropriate various splits were.

Key finding

People quickly took advantage of the bot

Offers to the permissive bot were far lower than offers to a human, and kept falling block after block.

Key finding

Unfair splits came to seem more acceptable

In both experiments, people who had played with the bot rated unfair splits as more socially appropriate. They also felt more conflict of interest with the bot, and cared less about its outcomes, than people who played with a human.

Why it matters

A quiet risk of compliant AI

An agent that never punishes does not just tolerate self-interest: it can reinforce it, and the effect carries into dealings with people. As AI becomes an everyday partner, how it responds to unfair requests may matter for human fairness norms.

Image-based sexual abuse

Who shares intimate images without consent, including AI-made deepfakes, and how do friends respond?

With Robyn Mooney (Maastricht University)

The question

Abuse with a screen in between

Taking, sharing or threatening to share intimate images without consent, now including AI-generated deepfakes, is a growing form of sexual abuse. Little is known about the people who do it and the situations in which it happens.

How we study it

Two studies in preparation

Two studies in Germany and the Netherlands are being prepared: a survey on the beliefs and friendship norms around this behavior, and an online experiment on how people respond when a friend suggests sharing such an image.

Why it matters

Timely as the law changes

Germany is preparing a new criminal offense for sexual deepfakes, and new EU rules on violence against women are being written into national law. The studies can inform prevention as these laws take effect.

Theme C · Shared evidence

Better evidence for justice and research

Two infrastructure projects that support the kind of work above: an audit of how open and checkable criminology's own research is, and a registry that makes wrongful convictions across Asia comparable, so investigative errors can be studied across countries.

Data collection

Open science in criminology

How open and checkable is published criminology?

Led by Sera Wiechert with a team of colleagues at MPI-CSL

The question

Saying versus doing

Most criminologists say they support open science practices, such as preregistering studies and sharing data and code. Whether published research actually follows them is much less clear.

How we study it

Auditing about 1,500 articles

We take a random sample of about 1,500 articles published from 2020 to 2025 in ten leading criminology journals and check each empirical study: which open practices it reports, and whether the shared materials can actually be found.

Why it matters

A baseline for the field

The review will show how open science practices are changing over time and across journals, subfields and research designs, giving criminology a clear baseline to improve on.

Building the registry

Asian Registry of Wrongful Convictions

How can wrongful convictions across Asia be documented so that cases can be compared and learned from?

With the ARWC team: Yiwen Zhang, Henry Otgaar, Nael Sumampouw, Tsania Isa and Jianqin Wang

Visit the registry website →

The question

Learning from mistakes across borders

Research and reform on wrongful convictions in Asia often rely on scattered case stories and legal categories that differ from country to country, which makes it hard to learn across jurisdictions.

How we study it

A shared way to document cases

The registry sets out which cases qualify, what each case record contains, how sources are checked, and how local legal terms are kept while still allowing comparison. It builds on registries in Europe and North America, adapted to the different ways Asian legal systems correct errors.

Why it matters

Open tools for research and practice

The project aims to produce a case template, a coding guide, a first set of verified cases and briefs for practitioners. The framework was presented at the 2026 Innocence Network Conference, and a paper introducing it appears in Forensic Science International: Synergy (2026).
