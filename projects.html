Program 1 · Main program

Partners in Crime

Why do young people break rules together?

Much offending by young people happens in company. Partners in Crime studies what happens between friends at the moment of a decision: the nudges they give, the favors they owe and the company they choose. We built two games for this, Sardegna (a virtual-reality quiz played with a real friend) and Eccellenza (an online investment game), alongside surveys, scenario studies and an analysis of published court judgments.

Co-led with Jean-Louis van Gelder · Max Planck Institute for the Study of Crime, Security and Law

Sardegna: is it the company or the nudge?

Do friends lead us into rule-breaking just by being there, or by egging us on?

With Jean-Louis van Gelder and Wade C. Jacobsen · virtual-reality study in MAXLab, MPI-CSL

Preprint on CrimRxiv →

The question

Two ways peers may influence us

Peers may sway us just by being around, or by pointing out a chance to break the rules and pushing us to take it, which criminologists call instigation. The two are hard to separate in everyday life, and lab studies rarely involve real friends.

What we built

Sardegna: a VR quiz played with a real friend

Pairs of friends meet as look-alike avatars in a shared virtual room and play a quiz together for a cash prize.

Inside Sardegna: a quiz question with a timer (A), the answer buttons (B), the frosted screen that separates the friends in the final round (C), and the shared table with the friend's avatar (D).

Key finding

Players felt present, and present with their friend

Players felt present in the virtual room, and those paired with a friend felt they shared it with them. VR can recreate being with a friend convincingly enough to study peer influence experimentally.

Key finding

Encouragement tripled cheating; presence alone did not change it

In the final round, a short message encouraging players to cheat tripled cheating. A friend who was merely present made no difference.

Implications

A tool other researchers can use

Sardegna gives causal evidence among real friends that a minimal prompt is enough to trigger rule-breaking. The game is available to other researchers on request, and its messages, rewards and number of peers can be changed to test other forms of peer influence.

Choosing company, catching habits

Do we choose company that matches us, and does that choice shape what we do later?

With Jean-Louis van Gelder (MPI-CSL) and Wade C. Jacobsen (University of Maryland)

The study

Stay or switch?

Peer research usually treats our choice of friends as a nuisance to control for. We studied it as a decision. In two online experiments, 591 young adults played an investment game in which they could cheat, next to a partner (in fact a computer program) who always played honestly or always cheated. Every ten rounds they chose to stay or switch.

The Eccellenza game: only two of nine investments pay out big, so reporting big payoffs far more often than chance reveals cheating.

Key finding

People chose partners who behaved like them

Players who cheated more at the start, or scored lower on honesty, tended to stay with a dishonest partner and leave an honest one; more honest players did the reverse. Sorting that friendship studies see over months and years appeared within minutes.

Key finding

Dishonest partners increased cheating

Next to a dishonest partner, players cheated far more.

With a dishonest partner With an honest partner

Estimated cheating (big-payoff reports above the 22% expected by chance), Experiment 1. Partners were random in block 1; from block 2 on, players had chosen to stay or switch. Experiment 2 showed the same pattern.

Key finding

The effect lasted after the partner left

The longer players had spent with a dishonest partner, the more they cheated when playing alone right afterwards. Part of the link between personal tendencies and cheating ran through these choices: who you stay with shapes what you are exposed to, and then what you do.

Implications

Choosing company and being influenced are linked

Choosing company and being influenced by it are linked steps in one process. Where people start also matters: switching was rare, so those who began with a dishonest partner spent about twice as long with one.

Forever in your debt

Does a favor make us follow a partner's lead in breaking rules, or simply keep us around?

With Sophie Hetche and Linda Rosar (MPI-CSL)

The study

Do favors make people follow a partner into rule-breaking?

Small favors make people more willing to comply with later requests. Does this extend to a partner's advice to break a rule? 304 young adults played online in real pairs, with no deception, each paired with someone who had given the opposite advice: follow the rule, or take money meant for others. Some received a favor when their better-off partner shared a larger starting pot.

Key finding

~50%

longer before leaving a partner who had done them a favor

Favors kept partnerships together

A favor made people feel grateful, indebted and obliged toward their partner, and they stayed with that partner longer.

Key finding

Partners moved toward each other, with or without a favor

Paired with someone of the opposite view, people moved toward their partner and drifted back once alone. But a favor did not make them follow their partner's advice more closely, contrary to classic reciprocity research.

Gain-seekers, paired with a rule-follower Rule-followers, paired with a gain-seeker

Average amount taken per decision (GBP), among the 194 participants from all conditions who later left their partner (exploratory). Gain-seekers had advised taking the money; rule-followers had advised against it.

Key finding

Unexpectedly, favor recipients took more

People who received a favor took more money overall, most clearly when their partner favored taking it. Because takings were shared, some may have seen taking as a way to repay the partner: “I wanted to help us to get more money.” This explanation is still tentative.

Favor recipients were also more likely to describe their choices in terms of shared gain (19% vs 5% of those who started equal).

Implications

Favors affected loyalty more than obedience

A favor did not make people follow their partner's advice, but it kept them in the partnership longer and may have made them more willing to bend rules for it. Favors may therefore keep people in risky partnerships, which limits classic ideas about reciprocity.

Which friends can talk us into it?

Which features of a friendship make us go along with a friend's request to break the rules?

With Jean-Louis van Gelder

The study

Which friends do we go along with?

Theories of crime say friends matter, but not which features of a friendship make us go along with a friend's request. 416 young adults named a close, a moderate and a casual friend, imagined each asking them to steal, get into a fight or break traffic rules, rated how likely they were to go along, and said how they would treat the friend afterwards.

Key finding

Three features of a friendship mattered

Having broken rules together before

Linked to more willingness

The friend has more power in the friendship

Linked to more willingness

Expecting the friendship to last

Linked to more willingness

How long you have been friends

No clear effect

How close you feel

No clear effect

How often you argue

No clear effect

Comparisons within each person across their three friends, also accounting for self-control, past delinquency and gender. Answers concern imagined situations, not observed behavior.

Key finding

Expecting the friendship to last mattered

How long a friendship had lasted made no difference; expecting it to last did. People were more willing to go along with friends they saw as part of their future. Friends with more power also made the act itself seem more acceptable.

Key finding

Going along did not always mean approving

Saying yes and feeling good about the friend usually went together, but not always. In about 8% of cases, people would go along while planning to think less of the friend or see them less, and many who refused kept the friendship intact.

Implications

What the friendship is like matters

Classic theories focus on how long and how often we spend time with friends. Our results point to what the friendship is like: shared rule-breaking, unequal power and the expectation of a lasting bond. Requests from such friends may be the hardest to refuse.

How courts treat peer influence in youth group offending

When young people offend together, do Dutch courts treat the influence of others as lessening their blame?

Based on 1,200 published judgments from rechtspraak.nl

The study

Do courts see peers the way research does?

Most youth crime is committed with others, and developmental research holds that peer influence lowers adolescents' blame. We coded 1,200 published Dutch criminal judgments from 2010 to 2025: 800 youth cases, 100 cases of young adults and 300 adult cases. Coding was automated with fixed codebooks; human coders are now checking it against the Dutch originals.

Preliminary analyses

Peer influence came up far more often in youth cases

Peer influence was raised in about half of youth group cases and in one in seven adult ones. Courts took it up in their own reasoning far less often.

It came through reports and served risk assessment

Implications

Risk rather than blame

In published Dutch judgments, peer influence is treated as a question of risk rather than of blame. English sentencing guidelines name peer pressure as a reason for a lighter sentence; Dutch law has no such rule. Reports could record who asked, pressured or recruited the young person, so that courts can weigh being led as part of blame.

PhD project · manuscript in preparation

Choosing and following the wrong crowd

Does living for the moment make young people more open to peer pressure?

Short-Term Mindsets Theory proposes that many risk factors for crime share one root: focusing on immediate rewards over long-term consequences, a mindset that can shift with circumstances. A structured review asks whether it makes adolescents more open to delinquent peers and shapes who they spend time with. The review will guide an online experiment.

Linda Rosar, PhD candidate, with Jean-Louis van Gelder

PhD project · data collection

When love encourages rule-breaking

Do romantic partners draw each other into rule-breaking?

Most research on peer influence looks at friends, but romantic partners may matter just as much. A survey asks young adults whether a partner ever drew them into breaking a rule, in which areas of life, and how often. The project also includes a meta-analysis and a planned virtual-reality study.

Sophie Hetche, PhD candidate, with Jean-Louis van Gelder

Program 2 · Main program

School investigations

How do teachers question students, and what happens to what they hear?

When a student is accused of bullying, theft or violence, a teacher or principal usually questions the students involved, often without any training. What the students say decides who is disciplined, whether parents and social services are informed and whether the police are called. Most incidents never reach the police, yet suspension and referral to youth justice are linked to later arrest, and discipline falls unequally on minority and disabled students. This program studies that invisible interview: how school staff question students, what they write down, how a complaints committee judges their inquiries, and what courts do with the information schools pass on.

Led by Yikang Zhang · Max Planck Institute for the Study of Crime, Security and Law

How Dutch secondary schools investigate misconduct

What do the staff who handle these cases actually do?

The question

The interview no one sees

When a student is accused of misconduct, someone at school questions the students involved and decides what to do next. Research on interviewing children and suspects has focused on police and trained interviewers. Almost nothing is known about how school staff question students in Europe.

How we study it

A survey of school staff

We are surveying the staff who handle these cases, such as care coordinators, confidential advisers and heads of department, about whom they talk to, which questions they ask, what they record and when they involve the police.

Coding underway

How schools find out

How do Dutch schools establish what happened when a pupil is suspected of misconduct, and how does an independent committee judge their inquiry?

Parents, pupils and staff who object to how a school handled an incident can complain to the National Education Complaints Committee, which publishes its opinions. We code all opinions published from 1998 to 2026: how the school gathered the facts, how the pupil was heard and the parents informed, when the police came in, which measures followed, and which standards the committee applied. The study covers the step before a case reaches court.

A case from the opinions

In one opinion, a secondary-school pupil suspected of stalking a classmate was taken out of class without notice and confronted with his accuser. His request to give his account separately was refused, and his parents were not told. The committee upheld the complaint: the school had not heard both sides and had not stayed neutral.

Based on published opinions of the National Education Complaints Committee (LKC)

When school matters reach the courtroom

What do Dutch youth courts do with what schools tell them about an incident and about the young person?

Based on published judgments from rechtspraak.nl

The study

From school to court

School information reaches Dutch youth courts as evidence about an incident (a teacher saw the fight, the register shows an absence) or as background on the young person, usually from youth probation or the Child Protection Board. The main study reads 37 published judgments in full, coding what school information is used for, whether the court examines it, and who passes it on. Preliminary analyses used a separate set.

Preliminary analyses

Courts explain where a point is disputed

Courts seem to give reasons about school information where someone disputes it, whether deciding guilt or sentence. Undisputed information sits in a list of evidence or inside a professional's report the court adopts. At sentencing the school rarely speaks for itself: judgments seldom say who at school said what, or how the report's author knows.

Preliminary; the main study will test this.

Preliminary analyses

Giving reasons did not always mean checking the source

When courts do give reasons, some examine the school source itself, such as how far away the teacher stood or how clear the camera images were. Others take the school's information as given and reason about something else. Counting both as "checked" would overstate how closely courts look at what schools report.

Preliminary; the main study will test this.

Preliminary analyses

Courts treated school discipline in different ways

When a school suspends or removes a pupil, judgments tend to mention it without saying who decided or why. Some courts count it as a burden already carried and lower the sentence; others do not take it up at all. So unexamined school information does not always work against the young person.

Preliminary; the main study will test this.

Implications

What comes next

Schools investigate, discipline and report. If these patterns hold, what a school says can shape a sentence without the judgment showing where it came from. The main study will test each pattern on its own set of judgments, and may confirm or contradict them.

Gasliteracy: when is a memory conflict gaslighting?

How do people tell everyday memory disagreements apart from gaslighting?

With Celine van Golde (University of Sydney)

Preprint on PsyArXiv →

The study

Gaslighting or ordinary disagreement?

Gaslighting, abuse that makes someone doubt their own memory, was Merriam-Webster's word of the year in 2022, yet partners disagree about what happened all the time. 252 adults recalled a time their partner contradicted their memory, defined gaslighting in their own words, rated how gaslit they had felt and described their relationship. Independent coders rated each incident.

Key finding

1 in 4

saw their own memory conflict as gaslighting, even to some extent

Memory conflicts were common; calling them gaslighting was not

Nearly two in three eligible participants could recall a memory conflict with their partner. Most saw it as an ordinary disagreement.

Key finding

Feeling gaslit went with a strained relationship

Felt more gaslit

Felt more gaslit

Felt more gaslit

Felt more gaslit

No clear link

No clear link

Associations with participants' own ratings of how gaslit they felt during the recalled conflict (251 participants).

Key finding

Coders and participants agreed only moderately

Independent coders agreed only moderately with participants. Relationship quality predicted both, but more strongly participants' own ratings: people in strained relationships may experience more gaslighting, read ambiguous conflicts that way, or both. Those who define gaslighting as deliberate manipulation set a higher bar for using the word.

Implications

Separating abuse from ordinary memory errors

Research and public communication need to separate gaslighting from everyday memory fallibility, so that real abuse is recognized without every disagreement being labeled abuse.

From gaslighter to gaslightee

Does it matter whose side of a memory conflict we see?

With Celine van Golde (University of Sydney)

The question

Does the point of view matter?

People can often tell gaslighting from ordinary disagreement. But does that depend on whether they picture themselves as the one challenging a partner's memory, or the one being challenged?

How we study it

Scenarios told from both sides

249 adults read memory-conflict scenarios that were either manipulative (a partner challenges a memory to avoid looking bad and keep control) or an honest disagreement, told from the challenger's side or the receiving side. They rated each from 1 (not gaslighting) to 8 (definitely gaslighting).

Key finding

Manipulation was easier to see from the challenger's side

Manipulative scenarios were rated as more gaslighting overall, but mainly when told by the challenger, whose motive was stated. From the receiving side, manipulation and honest disagreement looked much alike.

Rated the scenario as gaslighting (5 or more on the 1–8 scale, %)

Manipulative, told by the challenger

59%

Manipulative, told by the person challenged

47%

Honest disagreement, told by the person challenged

44%

Honest disagreement, told by the challenger

32%

0%25%50%75%100%

Each participant read two of the four scenarios.

Key finding

Definitions of gaslighting shaped the ratings

People who defined gaslighting as necessarily deliberate were less likely to call any scenario gaslighting; those who focused on harm to the victim were more likely to. Relationship quality made no difference, while higher dark-trait scores went with higher ratings of the manipulative challenger's story.

Implications

Manipulation is hard to see from the receiving end

From the receiving end, a partner's manipulative intent is rarely visible, which may be one reason gaslighting is hard to recognize while it is happening. Clear public definitions need to account for that.

Alcohol, bystanders and memory

How does alcohol change what bystanders do, and what witnesses later remember?

Alcohol may make it harder to notice risk and step in when someone is sexually harassed, and it is often involved when witnesses later say what happened. Sober and intoxicated participants experience a virtual pub in which harassment unfolds; we record how they react, then test their memory of the event.

Co-led with Sera Wiechert, with Jean-Louis van Gelder (MPI-CSL)

No bad deed goes punished

What happens to people whose unfair behavior goes unpunished?

With Qian Sun (Suzhou University of Science and Technology)

The study

Letting unfairness go

Punishing unfair behavior helps keep fairness norms alive, yet people often let it go. What does that do to the person who acted unfairly? 200 participants repeatedly split 100 points with a partner who either accepted nearly every offer or rejected unfair ones. Before and after, they made offers to a new partner and rated how appropriate different splits were.

Key finding

Lower offers continued with a new partner

Facing a partner who never punished, participants offered less during the game and later offered less to a new partner too. Those facing a partner who rejected unfair offers stayed fair.

Partner who accepted nearly everything Partner who rejected unfair offers

Average offer, out of 100 points, to a new partner whose response they did not see. During the game itself, offers averaged 33 versus 49 points.

Key finding

18% vs 4%

of later offers to a new partner were 20 points or less, after a permissive versus an enforcing partner

Very unfair offers became more common

Most people kept making fair offers, but very low offers became far more common after facing a partner who let everything slide.

Key finding

Unfair splits came to seem more acceptable

Participants who met a permissive partner rated moderately unfair splits as more socially appropriate, and became more willing to accept unfair offers themselves.

Implications

How fairness norms may erode

Letting unfairness slide changes what the person does next, toward other people, and what they think is acceptable. Repeated across many people and situations, this could slowly wear fairness norms down.

Artificial Enablers

Does dealing with AI that never pushes back make us less fair to people?

With Qian Sun (Suzhou University of Science and Technology)

The study

A partner that accepts everything

AI systems are built to be helpful and compliant; unlike people, they do not refuse unfair treatment. In two experiments registered in advance, 593 young adults repeatedly split points with a human partner or a simple bot (a program, not real AI) that accepted every offer. Afterwards they played with a new human and rated how appropriate various splits were.

Key finding

People quickly took advantage of the bot

Offers to the permissive bot were far lower than offers to a human, and kept falling block after block.

Key finding

In the second experiment, lower offers carried over to people

After playing with the bot, participants offered less to a new human partner; after playing with a human, they offered slightly more.

Played with the permissive bot Played with a human

Experiment 2. Average offer, out of 100 points, to a new human partner whose response they did not see. Experiment 1 used a different measure and found no clear carry-over.

Key finding

Unfair splits came to seem more acceptable

In both experiments, people who had played with the bot rated unfair splits as more socially appropriate. They also felt more conflict of interest with the bot, and cared less about its outcomes, than people who played with a human.

Implications

A possible risk of compliant AI

An agent that never punishes can reinforce self-interest, and in our second experiment the effect carried over to people. As people deal with AI more often, how it responds to unfair requests may matter for fairness between people.

Image-based sexual abuse

Who shares intimate images without consent, including AI-made deepfakes, and how do friends respond?

Taking, sharing or threatening to share intimate images without consent is a growing form of sexual abuse. Two studies in Germany and the Netherlands are in preparation: a survey on the beliefs and friendship norms around it, and an experiment on how people respond when a friend suggests sharing such an image. They come as Germany prepares an offense for sexual deepfakes and new EU rules take effect.

Theme C · Shared evidence

Better evidence for justice and research

Infrastructure for the work above: an audit of how open criminology's own research is, and a registry that makes wrongful convictions across Asia comparable across countries.

Data collection

Open science in criminology

How open and checkable is published criminology?

Most criminologists say they support practices such as preregistration and sharing data and code. We audit a random sample of about 1,500 articles published from 2020 to 2025 in ten leading criminology journals: which open practices each reports, and whether the shared materials can be found. The audit will show where the field stands.

Led by Sera Wiechert with a team of colleagues at MPI-CSL

Building the registry

Asian Registry of Wrongful Convictions

How can wrongful convictions across Asia be documented so that cases can be compared and learned from?

With the ARWC team: Yiwen Zhang, Henry Otgaar, Nael Sumampouw, Tsania Isa and Jianqin Wang

Visit the registry website →

The question

Scattered cases, different legal categories

Research and reform on wrongful convictions in Asia often rely on scattered case stories and legal categories that differ from country to country, which makes it hard to learn across jurisdictions.

How we study it

A shared way to document cases

The registry sets out which cases qualify, what each record contains, how sources are checked, and how local legal terms are kept while allowing comparison. It builds on registries in Europe and North America, adapted to how Asian legal systems correct errors.

Implications

Planned outputs

The project aims to produce a case template, a coding guide, a first set of verified cases and briefs for practitioners. The framework was presented at the 2026 Innocence Network Conference and is introduced in Forensic Science International: Synergy (2026).
